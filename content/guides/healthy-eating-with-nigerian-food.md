You do not need foreign food to eat well. Rice, beans, yam, plantain, soups, vegetables and fish can all fit into a healthy way of eating. What matters most is the balance on your plate, how the food is cooked, how much you eat, and how much hidden salt, sugar and oil comes with it.

This guide turns the World Health Organization's healthy diet advice into everyday choices for Nigerian kitchens. It is general information, not a personal diet plan.

## Start with the four principles

The World Health Organization describes a healthy diet with four principles:

- **Adequacy:** enough of the nutrients your body needs
- **Balance:** the energy you eat is balanced with the energy you use, with a sensible mix of carbohydrates, protein and fats
- **Moderation:** limited amounts of foods that harm health when eaten in excess, such as those high in free sugars, salt and unhealthy fats
- **Diversity:** a wide variety of foods within and across food groups

For weight management, balance is the key idea. The NHS explains that you lose weight when you eat and drink fewer calories than your body uses, and that steady loss of about 0.5 to 1 kg a week is a realistic aim.

## Build a balanced Nigerian plate

A simple way to apply these principles is to think about your plate in parts:

- **Vegetables first.** Fill a large part of the plate with vegetables: ugu, efo (spinach), bitter leaf, okra, garden egg, cabbage, carrots, tomatoes and peppers. The WHO advises adults to eat at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day.
- **A moderate portion of starch.** Rice, yam, plantain, bread and swallows such as eba, amala, fufu and pounded yam are energy-dense. You do not have to stop eating them, but the portion size matters.
- **A source of protein.** Fish, eggs, beans, chicken without the skin, lean meat and moi moi all count. The WHO notes that many adults may benefit from getting more of their protein from plants, such as beans and lentils.
- **A small amount of oil.** Use oil to cook, not to swim in.

## Choose better carbohydrates

The WHO advises that most carbohydrates should come from whole grains, vegetables, fruits and pulses. It lists unprocessed maize, millet, oats and brown rice as examples of whole grains, and beans, lentils and dried peas as pulses.

Practical swaps:

- Try brown rice or local unpolished rice in place of some white rice.
- Choose oats, pap made from whole maize or millet, or beans for breakfast more often than white bread and sugary cereal.
- Eat beans and moi moi regularly. Beans add protein and fibre. If they cause bloating, start with smaller portions.
- Keep swallows to a modest portion and pair them with a vegetable-rich soup.

The WHO also recommends at least 25 g of naturally occurring fibre a day for adults. Vegetables, beans, whole grains and fruit are the easiest ways to get there.

## Watch the oil, especially in soups and fried food

Fat is essential, but the type and amount matter. The WHO lists palm oil among the fats high in saturated fat, alongside butter, coconut oil and fatty meat. It advises keeping saturated fat below 10% of daily energy and suggests these changes:

- steam or boil instead of frying where you can
- use less oil in stews and soups, and choose oils rich in unsaturated fat, such as soybean or sunflower oil, for some of your cooking
- trim visible fat from meat and remove chicken skin
- limit fried snacks and baked goods such as puff-puff, chin chin, meat pies and biscuits

Fried foods like akara, dodo and fried yam can stay on the menu as occasional foods rather than daily ones.

## Cut hidden salt from stock cubes

The WHO advises adults to eat less than 5 g of salt a day. It names stock cubes and bouillon as common sources of hidden salt added during cooking, along with salty snacks and processed meats.

In many Nigerian kitchens, the biggest win is the stock cube. Try using fewer cubes per pot, adding flavour with crayfish, onions, garlic, ginger, pepper and fresh herbs, and tasting before you add more salt.

## Drink less sugar

The WHO advises keeping free sugars below 10% of daily energy, which is about 50 g or 12 level teaspoons a day for an average adult, and suggests that going below 5% brings extra benefit. Free sugars include sugar added to food and drinks and the sugars in fruit juice, honey and syrups.

Simple changes:

- choose water over soft drinks and malt drinks most of the time
- treat fruit juice as an occasional drink, even when it says no added sugar, and eat whole fruit instead
- add less sugar to tea, pap and oats, and cut down over a few weeks so your taste adjusts

## Sample day of balanced Nigerian meals

This is an example, not a prescription. Adjust portions to your appetite, activity and any advice from your doctor.

- **Breakfast:** oats or pap with less sugar and a boiled egg, or moi moi with a small portion of pap
- **Lunch:** a modest portion of brown or local rice with vegetable-rich stew and fish
- **Snack:** a piece of fruit, such as an orange, a small banana or a slice of watermelon, or a handful of groundnuts
- **Dinner:** a small portion of swallow with okra, efo riro or ugu soup made with less oil and fewer stock cubes, plus fish or lean meat

## Eating out and at parties

Owambe and work events are part of life. A few habits help: fill up on the protein and vegetables first, take one portion of rice instead of mixing jollof, fried rice and small chops, choose water or zero-sugar drinks, and eat slowly. The NHS also notes that alcohol is high in calories, so cutting down helps with weight management.

## Move more

Food is only part of the picture. The NHS recommends being active for 45 to 60 minutes a day when trying to lose weight, and you can break that into shorter sessions. Brisk walking counts.

## When to get professional advice

Speak to a doctor before you make big changes to your diet if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, living with diabetes, high blood pressure, kidney disease or another medical condition, or taking regular medication. A doctor or a registered dietitian can give you advice that fits your health and budget. If bloating is your main concern, read our guide on [bloating or belly fat](/guides/bloating-or-belly-fat).

## The bottom line

Healthy eating with Nigerian food is about balance, not giving up the meals you grew up with. Fill a large part of your plate with vegetables, keep starchy portions moderate, add protein, cook with less oil and fewer stock cubes, and drink less sugar. Small, steady changes are easier to keep than strict diets.
