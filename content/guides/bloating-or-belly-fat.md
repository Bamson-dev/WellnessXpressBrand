A tummy that looks bigger than usual can mean two very different things. It can be bloating, which is mostly gas or fluid in the gut and changes from hour to hour. Or it can be fat stored around the waist, which builds up slowly and stays. The two need different responses, so it helps to know which one you are dealing with.

This guide explains the signs of each, what usually causes bloating, simple habits that ease it, and the warning signs that mean you should see a doctor.

## The quick difference

**Bloating changes during the day.** Your tummy may feel flat in the morning and tight or swollen after meals. It often comes with discomfort, rumbling, or passing more wind than usual.

**Belly fat stays the same from morning to night.** It does not swell after one meal and shrink a few hours later. It changes slowly, over weeks and months, as your weight changes.

A simple check: if your waistband feels very different at 7am and at 7pm, bloating is the likely cause. If it feels the same all day, every day, you are more likely looking at stored fat.

## Signs that point to bloating

The NHS lists these common signs of bloating:

- your tummy feels full or bigger than usual
- you have tummy pain or discomfort
- your tummy is rumbling or making noises
- you are passing wind more than usual

Bloating often follows a pattern. Many people notice it after large meals, fizzy drinks, eating quickly, or certain foods. Some women feel bloated around the time of their period.

## What usually causes bloating

According to the NHS, the most common reason for bloating is a lot of gas in the gut. That gas can come from:

- some foods and drinks, such as certain vegetables and fizzy drinks
- swallowing air while you eat, for example when you eat fast or talk while chewing

Bloating can also come from a problem with digestion, such as constipation, a food intolerance, coeliac disease or irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). These need a proper diagnosis, so do not guess. If bloating keeps coming back, talk to a doctor.

## Habits that help ease bloating

The NHS suggests these everyday steps:

- exercise regularly, which helps digestion
- chew with your mouth closed so you swallow less air
- drink plenty of water
- if you are constipated, eat foods high in soluble fibre, such as oats
- eat smaller, more frequent meals instead of one large meal

It also suggests cutting down on:

- fizzy drinks, alcohol, and caffeine in coffee and tea
- large amounts of foods known to cause gas, such as cabbage, beans and lentils
- big meals late at night, and slouching while you eat
- processed, sugary, spicy or fatty foods

For many Nigerian homes, the practical version is simple. Eat your largest meal earlier in the day where you can. Slow down at the table. Swap some soft drinks for water. If beans or certain vegetables give you gas, eat smaller portions and notice how your body responds, rather than cutting out healthy foods completely.

## Signs that point to belly fat

Fat around the waist builds up when you regularly take in more energy from food and drink than your body uses. The NHS notes that common drivers include low physical activity and eating and drinking more than the body needs, especially foods and drinks high in fat and sugar.

A useful home check is your **waist-to-height ratio**. The NHS advises keeping your waist size to less than half your height. For example, if you are 160 cm tall, aim for a waist under 80 cm. Measure your waist halfway between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hips, after breathing out normally.

The NHS also points out that people from a Black African or African-Caribbean background face health risks at a slightly lower body mass index (BMI) than other groups. That makes the waist check especially useful.

## What helps with belly fat

There is no food, drink or product that removes fat from one spot. Fat around the waist reduces as part of overall, steady weight loss. The NHS recommends:

- eating a healthy, balanced diet with plenty of fruit and vegetables, and less high-fat and sugary food
- being active for 45 to 60 minutes a day, which you can split into shorter sessions
- aiming for steady weight loss of about 0.5 to 1 kg a week
- cutting down on alcohol
- setting realistic goals and checking your progress regularly

The World Health Organization adds that diets built on whole grains, vegetables, fruits and pulses, with limited free sugars, salt and unhealthy fats, support long-term health. ## When to see a doctor

Bloating is usually harmless, but not always. The NHS advises seeing a doctor if:

- you feel bloated regularly
- you have changed your diet and still keep feeling bloated
- you have bloating along with weight loss you did not plan
- you have bloating and blood in your stool

Get urgent medical help, at a clinic or hospital, if bloating comes with vomiting, a high temperature, a lump or swelling in your tummy, severe or sudden tummy pain, or if you cannot pass stool or wind. Bloating that does not go away can sometimes be a sign of a more serious condition, so do not ignore it.

Speak to a doctor or pharmacist before you try any supplement or remedy for bloating, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication or living with a medical condition.

## The bottom line

Bloating rises and falls, often with meals, and usually eases with slower eating, smaller portions and fewer fizzy drinks. Belly fat stays put and only reduces with steady, overall weight loss. If you are not sure which one you have, or if bloating keeps coming back, a doctor can help you find the cause.
